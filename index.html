Sounding Line Ocean conservation, by depth

The oceanis not scenery

It is the largest working part of the climate system, and it is the part we understand least. This page descends through it — one zone at a time — to show what the ocean does for us, what is breaking, and what has measurably worked.

Five zones · 0 to 6 000 metres · then back to the light

Zone 01 · Epipelagic 0 – 200 m

Everything that lives here, lives on light

The sunlit layer is a thin skin — two hundred metres on an ocean that averages nearly four thousand. Almost all photosynthesis in the sea happens in it. The drifting plants that do that work are microscopic, and between them they run a share of the planet's life support that is easy to underestimate.

71% of Earth's surface is ocean, holding about 97% of its water. NOAA
~50% of the oxygen in the atmosphere is produced by marine phytoplankton — at minimum. NOAA
>90% of the excess heat trapped by greenhouse gases has been absorbed by the ocean. IPCC AR6
~25% of annual human CO2 emissions are taken up by seawater each year. Global Carbon Project

Every one of those services is a subsidy. None of them appears on a balance sheet, and each of them has a limit.

Zone 02 · Mesopelagic 200 – 1 000 m

The pressures do not arrive one at a time

Below the light, the water still moves carbon downward — small fish and plankton rise to feed at night and sink by day, carrying carbon with them. It is one of the largest biological migrations on Earth, and it happens twice a day, everywhere. It is also where four separate pressures meet.

Heat

Marine heatwaves are no longer episodes

Ocean heat content has set successive records. Warmer water holds less oxygen and less dissolved carbon dioxide, stratifies more strongly, and shifts species poleward faster than fishing rules can follow.

Chemistry

Surface pH has fallen about 0.1 units since the industrial era

Because pH is logarithmic, that is roughly a 30% increase in acidity. It makes building shells and skeletons measurably harder for corals, oysters, and the pteropods near the base of many food webs.

Fishing

Roughly a third of assessed stocks are fished beyond sustainable limits

FAO's 2024 assessment puts it at 37.7%. The pressure is now moving downward: proposals to trawl the mesopelagic for fishmeal would target the same animals that run the biological carbon pump.

Plastic

An estimated 8–11 million tonnes enter the ocean every year

Most published estimates fall in that band. The visible fraction is the smaller problem; fragments below a millimetre are now found in sea ice, deep-sea sediment, and the tissue of animals at every trophic level.

Zone 03 · Bathypelagic 1 000 – 4 000 m

No sunlight has ever reached this water

Past a thousand metres the last blue is gone. What light there is, the animals make themselves — bioluminescence is the ordinary condition here, not the exception. This is the largest habitat on the planet by volume, and the least surveyed.

The reefs above it are the clearest ledger of what warming costs. Corals and their algae separate under heat stress; the coral bleaches white and, if the heat holds, starves.

4th global bleaching event on record, running from 2023 across all three ocean basins. NOAA Coral Reef Watch
~50% of the world's living coral cover has been lost since the 1950s. Eddy et al., 2021
~25% of all marine species depend on reefs, which cover well under 1% of the seafloor. UNEP
~3bn people rely on seafood for a significant share of their animal protein. FAO

Zone 04 · Abyssopelagic 4 000 – 6 000 m

The decision being made in the dark

The abyssal plain is the largest single habitat on Earth. It is also carpeted, in places, with polymetallic nodules — potato-sized lumps of manganese, nickel, and cobalt that took millions of years to form and that several companies would like to collect at industrial scale.

The ecological case against moving quickly is unusually simple: the sediment plumes raised by collection would settle over areas far larger than those mined, and the communities living on those nodules have no demonstrated way to recolonise on any human timescale. Test tracks cut into the seafloor in the 1970s and 1980s are still visible, still largely unrecovered.

More than thirty governments have called for a pause or moratorium until the science is settled. The International Seabed Authority has not yet adopted a completed mining code. This one is still open.

Zone 05 · Hadal 6 000 m and below

Even here, the record is ours

The deepest surveyed trenches lie near eleven kilometres down, at pressures over a thousand times those at the surface. Fewer people have visited the bottom of the Mariana Trench than have walked on the Moon.

The amphipods living there carry industrial pollutants — PCBs and persistent organic compounds banned decades ago — at concentrations comparable to some of the most contaminated coastal rivers on Earth. Plastic bags have been photographed at the bottom. There is no part of the ocean that is somewhere else.

Which is the argument for turning around.

Ascent What has measurably worked

Protection is the one intervention with a track record

Conservation reporting tends to stop at the damage. But the ocean is unusually responsive: fully protected areas accumulate fish biomass several times over in a decade, and the surplus spills across their borders into the fisheries next door. The problem is not that protection fails. It is that most of what gets counted as protection does not restrict anything.

Law

The High Seas Treaty closed a two-thirds gap

Adopted in June 2023 after nearly twenty years of negotiation, the BBNJ agreement creates, for the first time, a legal route to establish protected areas in international waters — the two-thirds of the ocean that belonged to no one's rulebook.

Recovery

Populations come back when the pressure stops

Humpback whales in the Southern Hemisphere have rebuilt from a few percent of their pre-whaling numbers. Several rebuilt stocks — including Atlantic swordfish and Pacific bluefin — show the same shape. Recovery is slow, unglamorous, and repeatable.

Carbon

Blue carbon does two jobs at once

Mangroves, seagrass meadows, and salt marshes bury carbon in waterlogged sediment at rates per hectare far above tropical forest, and hold it for centuries. They are also nurseries and storm buffers. Restoring them is among the few climate actions that pays out locally and immediately.

Management

Science-based catch limits work where they are enforced

In regions that set quotas from stock assessments and actually police them, the share of overfished stocks has fallen. The failure is concentrated where monitoring is weakest — which makes enforcement, not new science, the binding constraint.

01

Push on the 30% target

National protection commitments are decided politically. Ask your representatives what share of your country's waters is fully protected — not merely designated.

02

Choose fisheries, not just species

How a fish was caught matters more than which fish it was. Regional seafood guides rank by fishery and gear type; use one at the counter.

03

Cut plastic upstream

Beach cleanups matter locally. Production and packaging rules matter at scale — support the ones on the table where you live.

04

Back the deep-sea pause

The seabed mining code is unfinished. That is exactly the window in which public pressure changes an outcome rather than commenting on one.
